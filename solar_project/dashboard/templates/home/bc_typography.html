Solar Energy Advanced Insights

Key definitions and billing concepts every solar homeowner should understand.

Core Definitions

Essential solar energy terms for understanding and monitoring your home system.

Power (W / kW)
The rate at which energy is produced or consumed at any given moment. Your solar panels are rated in kilowatts (kW) — an 8 kW system produces 8,000 watts under ideal sunlight.
Current (A)
The flow of electrical charge through a circuit, measured in Amperes. Think of it like the volume of water moving through a pipe — more current means more electricity flowing at once.
Voltage (V)
The electrical pressure that drives current through a circuit. Your home runs on 120V/240V. Solar panels produce DC voltage that your inverter converts to AC for home use.
Energy (kWh)
Power used or produced over time. Your electric bill is measured in kilowatt-hours. If your system produces 8 kW for 5 hours, that's 40 kWh generated that day — and what gets credited back to you.
Efficiency (%)
How well your panels convert sunlight into usable electricity. Most residential panels are 18–22% efficient. Your inverter also carries an efficiency rating, typically 95–98%.
Net Metering
A billing arrangement where excess solar energy you send to the grid earns credits on your electric bill — offsetting what you draw from the grid at night or on cloudy days.
Peak Sun Hours
Not the same as daylight hours. These are hours of sunlight intense enough for full panel output (≥1,000 W/m²). Texas averages 4.5–5.5 peak sun hours per day — used to estimate your daily production.
Inverter
The device that converts your panels' DC electricity into the AC electricity your home uses. String inverters handle your whole array; microinverters work panel-by-panel for better performance in shade.
Energy Company Buyback Policies

When your panels produce more than you use, that surplus flows to the grid. What happens next depends entirely on your utility's policy.

Full Net Metering
You receive a 1-for-1 bill credit at the full retail rate for every kWh you export. The best deal for homeowners — the grid effectively acts as a free battery. Ask your REP if this is available before signing.
Avoided-Cost Buyback
You receive credit for exports, but at the wholesale rate — typically 2–5¢/kWh compared to the 10–15¢/kWh retail rate you pay. Still a benefit, but your system's payback period will be longer.
No Compensation
Some utilities offer zero credit for exported energy. A few go further and charge standby or demand fees just for having solar connected to the grid. If your utility falls here, a home battery becomes significantly more valuable.
True-Up
An annual billing settlement where your accumulated net credits and charges are reconciled all at once. Common with formal net metering programs — credits may not pay out until year-end.
Interconnection Agreement
The contract between you and your utility that governs how your solar system connects to the grid. This document locks in your export terms — read it carefully before installation.
REP (Texas)
Retail Electric Provider — the company you choose in Texas's deregulated market. Texas has no statewide net metering law, so buyback policies vary widely. Always compare REP plans before going solar.
Common Misconceptions

Things that often trip up new solar owners

kW and kWh are not the same. kW measures power right now; kWh measures energy over time. Your bill is based on kWh — not how many kW your system is rated for.

More panels doesn't always mean more savings. If your utility doesn't credit excess energy, oversizing your system gives you little return on the extra cost.

Self-consumption — using your own solar directly — is always more financially valuable than exporting it to the grid, regardless of your utility's policy.

Peak sun hours in the Rio Grande Valley are among the highest in Texas, which means your system will consistently outperform estimates based on national averages.

Questions to Ask Your Utility
  • Do you offer net metering — at retail or wholesale rate?
  • Do unused credits roll over month to month?
    • Or do they expire at month-end?
    • Is there an annual true-up settlement?
    • What happens to leftover credits at year-end?
  • Are there standby, demand, or interconnection fees for solar customers?
  • Is your buyback policy locked in or subject to change?
  • Do you offer time-of-use rates that reward battery discharge at night?
Financial Priority Order
  1. Use your solar energy yourself (self-consumption)
  2. Store excess in a home battery if available
  3. Export to the grid only after the above
  4. Confirm your REP offers a solar buyback plan
  5. Compare their buyback rate vs. your retail rate
  6. Review your interconnection agreement carefully
  7. Reassess your plan if your utility's policy changes
Texas Solar Fast Facts
  • Texas has no statewide net metering mandate
  • ERCOT operates Texas's independent power grid
    • Deregulated — you choose your own REP
  • Solar-added home value is property-tax exempt in Texas
  • Federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC) is currently 30%

The Bottom Line

The financial return of your solar system comes down to two things: how much you produce, and how much of that production you're actually compensated for. Understanding your utility's policy — before you sign anything — is just as important as choosing the right panels.

The best solar investment isn't always the biggest system — it's the right-sized system paired with the best available buyback policy in your area.
